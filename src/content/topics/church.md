## Define the ministry and the money needed

Church fundraising can support a mission trip, youth activity, community meal, accessibility improvement, or another specific ministry need. Start with a budget that distinguishes the project from ordinary congregational giving. Explain why the campaign is being run, who will manage the proceeds, and what will happen if the goal is not reached. A clear purpose helps supporters understand the request without assuming it replaces existing commitments.

Coordinate dates with the congregation's regular giving calendar and other ministry appeals. Ask the responsible church leader or finance committee to approve the plan before volunteers collect orders or open an online campaign in the church's name.

## Select a format the congregation can sustain

A coffee or gift sale can work through existing relationships and scheduled gatherings. Plan how orders reach people who do not attend every week. An online merchandise campaign can connect a dispersed supporter community, while a donation page can explain a defined need without requiring a purchase. Ongoing shopping programs require someone to keep enrollment and reporting useful after the initial announcement.

Consider whether the fundraiser primarily serves congregants or also invites the surrounding community. A program that relies on weekly announcements has a different audience from a public event. Write an invitation that explains the purpose to someone unfamiliar with the church.

## Compare costs with the expected net contribution

There is no universal church fundraising margin. A product sale retains a share of purchases, while a donation campaign may deduct transaction or service charges. An event adds food, venue, equipment, or staffing expenses. Request a written explanation of each cost and when the congregation can access the proceeds.

Suppose a group needs $900 for supplies and expects 60 orders. Each order would have to produce $15 in net proceeds to cover that need before other expenses. This is an illustrative budget, not a typical result. If the required return is unrealistic, adjust the project, audience, or approach before asking volunteers to commit.

## Account for hospitality and volunteer capacity

An existing congregation offers familiar communication channels, but the same people may already serve on several committees. Estimate the full workload: setup, reminders, money handling, delivery, troubleshooting, and final reporting. Choose a smaller campaign that can be managed well rather than assuming volunteers will absorb every missing task.

Keep purchasing voluntary and provide accessible participation options. Avoid public lists that equate giving with commitment to the congregation. If the campaign supports a traveler, family, or outside organization, make the beneficiary clear and let church leadership determine the approved financial arrangement. Questions about receipts or deductibility should go to the church's authorized finance contact rather than a sales volunteer.

## Compare the listed programs and confirm eligibility

Giving Bean offers coffee and tea fundraising, Bonfire provides custom-merchandise campaigns, RaiseRight supports earnings through eligible purchases, and Givebutter offers online campaign tools. These approaches differ in audience, fulfillment, account setup, and payment timing. Use the profiles below to identify which questions to bring to each provider, then confirm the current requirements for your congregation.

Before launch, assign an adult account owner, a payment reviewer, and a contact for supporter questions. Afterward, report the net funds received and how they were used. A short, specific thank-you that connects the campaign to its ministry outcome gives the next fundraiser a more credible foundation than another generic appeal for support.
