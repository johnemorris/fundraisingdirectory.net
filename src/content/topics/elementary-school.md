## Start with a specific school need

An elementary school fundraiser works best when families can explain the goal in one sentence: replace playground equipment, buy classroom books, or cover a field trip. Price the project before selecting a program. Separate the amount the school needs from the total amount families would have to spend. A product sale collects revenue, but only part of that revenue becomes usable school funds.

Ask the principal and parent organization to coordinate a single calendar. Several classrooms selling different products at once can compete for the same households. Give families a clear start date, closing date, and explanation of who controls the money.

## Choose an activity families can manage

A schoolwide fun run or reading challenge can bring students together around an activity rather than a purchase. Plan an accessible way for every child to participate, including children whose families cannot donate. Confirm who supplies the event equipment, tracks participation, sends reminders, and handles weather changes.

A catalog or online product sale may suit a school with a strong parent communication network. Direct shipping can reduce sorting duties, while consolidated delivery requires a collection plan. For an individual classroom material request, a teacher project platform may be more appropriate than a campaign seeking unrestricted money for the parent organization.

## Work out proceeds before promising a result

There is no single reliable profit margin for elementary school fundraising. Event service levels, product selections, payment fees, prizes, and shipping can change the result. Ask for a written example showing what the school receives at a realistic participation level. Include any minimum sales requirement and the date the funds become available.

For illustration only, if 120 households each generate $15 in net proceeds, the school raises $1,800 before any additional school-paid costs. This is a planning calculation, not a forecast. Compare it with volunteer capacity and the project's actual deadline before committing to a larger target.

## Balance participation and workload

Activity campaigns can create a shared school experience, but they need supervision, space, and a plan for students with different abilities. Product campaigns give supporters a tangible purchase, but can create pressure on families and additional delivery work. Avoid measuring a child's contribution only by dollars raised or placing children in charge of collecting from unfamiliar adults.

Use school-approved communication channels and ask which student information may be shared with a provider. Assign one adult to reconcile receipts and another to review the final totals. Give families a private way to ask questions or decline sales participation without affecting their child's experience.

## Compare programs with the right purpose

Booster offers school activity fundraising, while Read-A-Thon centers on reading participation. Charleston Wrap provides a product-sale alternative. DonorsChoose serves eligible public-school teacher projects and fulfills approved resources; it is not a substitute for unrestricted PTO proceeds. The profiles below explain those differences and link to the underlying provider research.

Before selecting a program, agree on success measures beyond gross revenue: net proceeds, volunteer hours, delivery accuracy, and family feedback. After the campaign, publish a simple account of funds received and the purchase or project they supported. Keep those notes for next year's organizers so the next committee can make a better comparison.
