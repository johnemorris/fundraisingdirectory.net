## Choose a candy-selling model before ordering

Candy fundraising can mean selling stock the group already holds, collecting preorders, or sharing an online product store. Those formats create different responsibilities. A direct sale gives supporters an immediate purchase, but someone must receive the stock, distribute it to sellers, and account for what remains. Preorders can reduce guessing about demand while adding order-checking and later delivery tasks.

Start with the venue and audience. A team selling at an approved community event has a different opportunity from a classroom relying on family contacts. Confirm permission for the sale and choose a product and price that the intended supporters can reasonably consider.

## Plan inventory and money handling

For stock-based sales, record how much product each adult or participant receives and what must be returned. Agree on deadlines for payments and unsold items. Ask the provider who owns unsold stock, what return options exist, and which storage conditions apply. Do not treat an order as risk free until the written terms support that conclusion.

Keep sales records simple enough for volunteers to maintain consistently. Assign an adult to reconcile stock against receipts and another to review the total. For online orders, explain which organization appears at checkout, where the product ships, and whom buyers should contact if they need help after the campaign closes.

## Understand the difference between sales and profit

Candy fundraising margins depend on the product, quantity, payment terms, delivery, and other costs. A headline percentage does not tell you how much cash will remain if some stock is unsold. Ask for a written quote and calculate the result under both the planned sales total and a more conservative scenario.

For a hypothetical example, buying 200 items at $1 each and selling every item for $2 creates $400 in revenue and $200 before other costs. Selling only 150 leaves $100 in cash above the original purchase, plus 50 unsold items. These figures are illustrative, not a provider price or typical result. The example shows why inventory exposure matters alongside margin.

## Consider venue rules and supporter needs

A small, familiar product can be easy to explain, and an immediate sale may suit a permitted event table. The tradeoffs include storage, transport, cash reconciliation, ingredient questions, and the risk of buying more than supporters want. Chocolate products also require particular care in warm conditions; follow the manufacturer's guidance rather than improvising storage.

Check school or venue food-sale rules before launch, and make ingredient information available through the provider. Do not promise that a product is allergy safe or suitable for a specific diet without verified details. Give children supervised, age-appropriate roles and avoid making door-to-door selling or purchases a condition of participation in the group.

## Compare the direct sellers and an alternative

World's Finest Chocolate offers chocolate product-sale fundraising. Fundraising.com offers multiple sale formats and includes candy and chocolate products. Double Good is a popcorn alternative for groups that want to compare an online, direct-to-supporter model rather than holding candy stock. The profiles below distinguish these options; they are not a claim that all three sell the same products.

Before choosing, ask about minimum orders, payment dates, returns, and the person responsible for delivery problems. Afterward, record net proceeds and unsold quantities separately. A campaign that generates substantial sales but leaves volunteers with excess stock should be evaluated differently from one that meets a smaller goal with predictable work and no unresolved inventory.
